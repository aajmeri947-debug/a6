The Washing & Care Guide

Why most socks are destroyed in a machine, and the five habits that stop it.

Socks very rarely wear out at the heel first. They go baggy, stop staying up, and get thrown away with plenty of fabric left. That is the elastane failing, and heat is what does it.

Temperature

Thirty degrees, for everything. Nothing in a sock needs more: modern detergent works perfectly well cool, and the only thing a sixty degree wash achieves is breaking down the elastane in the cuff several times faster. If something genuinely needs sanitising, that is a specific situation rather than a weekly habit.

Hot washes and hot tumbling, together, can halve the working life of a drawer.

Drying

Air drying flat is kindest and takes a few hours in a warm room. A tumble dryer on low is a reasonable compromise. On high heat it is the single most destructive thing you can do to a sock, because heat and mechanical tumbling attack the elastane and the fibre at the same time.

Wool in particular should never be tumbled warm. Felting is permanent, and a felted sock has lost both its size and its ability to breathe.

Inside out, and sorted by fibre

The inside of a sock collects skin, oils and nearly all of the smell, so washing inside out puts the dirty surface against the water and keeps the outside looking newer. Sorting by fibre rather than colour matters more than most people expect: wool and bamboo want cool and gentle, cotton and synthetics do not mind, and running them together means everything gets the harshest cycle in the load.

Rotation

Elastane recovers between wears, which is why a rotation of eight pairs lasts far more than twice as long as a rotation of four. Five is the sensible minimum for whatever you wear most. Buying two identical multipacks rather than one also solves the odd-sock problem permanently.

Darning a heel in ten minutes

A thinning heel is worth ten minutes before it becomes a hole. Turn the sock inside out, put something round inside to hold the shape, and work rows of stitches across the thin patch, then across those at right angles. It does not need to be neat; it needs to be inside a shoe. The Lifespan Estimator will tell you roughly when to start checking.

Storing them

Folded flat or laid in pairs, never balled with one cuff stretched over the other. That rolled ball is convenient and it permanently stretches the cuff of the outer sock, which is the part you most need to keep its grip. Keep them somewhere dry; a damp drawer is how wool gets a smell nothing will shift.

And throw away the ones that no longer stay up. A sock that slides inside the shoe is the start of the blister risk covered in the Blister Risk Checker, not merely an annoyance.
