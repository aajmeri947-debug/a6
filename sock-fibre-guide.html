The Sock Fibre Guide

Reading the percentages on the packet, and knowing which of them you are actually paying for.

Every sock is a blend, and the blend is printed on the packet in order of quantity. Once you can read it, most of the marketing around socks becomes unnecessary.

The percentages, in order

The first fibre listed is the one you feel. The last one, almost always elastane at one to five per cent, is the one that decides whether the sock still stays up in a year. In between, nylon is usually doing the hard-wearing at the heel and toe.

A sock listed as 70% merino, 28% nylon, 2% elastane is a merino sock reinforced to survive a boot. One listed as 80% cotton, 18% polyester, 2% elastane is a cotton sock with a bit of structure. Both descriptions tell you more than any name on the label.

Cotton

Soft, cheap and familiar, and the wrong choice for anything active. Cotton absorbs a great deal of water and gives it up slowly, so it sits wet against the skin, and wet skin takes a fraction of the friction to blister that dry skin does.

For a seated day in a dress shoe it is perfectly pleasant. For a hike it is the fibre most likely to end the day badly.

The elastane is one or two per cent of the sock and decides nearly all of its working life.

Merino and wool

Merino is the most useful all-round sock fibre there is. It regulates temperature in both directions, keeps insulating when damp, and resists smell well enough that a pair can be worn twice without anyone minding. The fibre is fine, so a thin merino sock is less durable than a thick one, which is why most are blended with nylon.

Thicker traditional wool insulates by bulk instead. It is warmer and coarser, and it felts if washed warm, which is how most of them are ruined.

Nylon, polyester and bamboo

Nylon is the toughest common fibre and the reason the heel and toe of a good sock outlast the rest. Polyester wicks and dries fastest, which makes it the sensible choice for sport, at the cost of holding smell. Bamboo viscose is extraordinarily smooth and not durable; it belongs in a desk drawer rather than a boot.

All-day comfort in a boot60 to 75% merino, 20 to 35% nylon
Running or gymPolyester or nylon with a flat toe seam
A dress sock that lastsCombed cotton with at least 15% nylon
Warmth without bulkMid-weight merino, not thick wool
Softness above allBamboo, and accept the shorter life

Knit density and construction

Two socks with identical blends can differ in life by a year. Needle count decides how tightly the yarn is packed, a cushioned sole doubles the material at the point that wears, and the toe seam decides whether you feel a ridge. A flat or linked toe is worth looking for and costs almost nothing to make.

Turn a sock inside out in the shop. Loose, open knitting at the heel tells you where it will go first.

What is worth paying for

Merino content, nylon reinforcement at the heel and toe, a flat toe seam, and a cuff with enough elastane to hold without marking the leg. Not worth paying for: a brand name on the cuff, antibacterial treatments that wash out, or arch-support panels in an everyday sock.
